History

This language was created by a user known as Lohk in 2009. Initially, its name was Lohkdesgds, but it was later changed because the name ended up being used as his username in games and social networks rather than linked to the language.

In the beginning, the goal of the project was to have the ultimate language that no teacher could decipher and that students could use to talk across corners of the classroom. If it were original, there would be no issue if the teacher intercepted a note, since they wouldn't understand a thing.

In the same year, the basic rules for constructing the language were defined and the first tests were conducted. Unfortunately, the reception was terrible, and the language fell into disuse a few days after being created.

Today, this file lists the latest updates since 2009. The data here remains up to date, as Lohk still uses it every now and then, so this language will probably never die.

Lohk's message to everyone reading this file is as follows: found a word you'd like to use that doesn't have a translation yet? Send a message on Discord (@lohk) or Twitter (@lohkdesgds). An update should probably come out within a few days.

If many people collaborate, we will probably have a more complete language one day. It is flexible and currently only adapted or expanded by Lohk himself. Who knows, maybe one day we'll have enough people to make that happen?


Writing

There is great interest in an official way to write in this language. To make it easier to use, the standard chosen was the US keyboard layout, containing letters from A to Z, but a valid font is also Avali Scratch (you can change the font at the top of the page).

The format of the Avali Scratch font is quite interesting, as it is mostly based on strokes, so for some it might even be faster and easier to write (besides looking much more unique).

Basic rules of use

This language has a few simple and easy-to-remember rules:

  • Pronouns always have two letters;
  • Verbs will have three letters for the root, and 5 or 7 when conjugated or with a modifier;
  • General words usually have 4 letters and can have 6 in some cases;
  • The "inverse" or "opposite" of a word (like the prefix "un-" or "dis-", or "des-" in Portuguese, as in "undo") can be literally obtained by putting "inverse", or "mank", before the word. It's a simple way of speaking, though it might not be obvious at first if you aren't used to it. Example: "mank krk": to un-update ("opposite update").
  • Both "?" and "wu" can be used to highlight a question ("wu" makes it clearer when speaking that it is a question). Example: "Wu ae tue fuil?";
  • Generally, word order does not matter as long as idea blocks (groups of verbs and words that make sense together) stay together. Example: "Ue tue fuil" / "Fuil ue tue" / "Tue fuil ue". However, SVO (subject + verb + object) is preferred.

For pronunciation, the rules are also simple:

  • In general, reading sounds like a mix of Portuguese, English, and German;
  • Y always sounds like "ai" (like "eye");
  • Q without a vowel in front or at the end has a sound similar to queue or quack (in English, the "qu" part). When followed by a vowel, it sounds like the "c" in "cat", "cot"...;
  • C always sounds like Q without a vowel—that is, like queue or quack (the "qu" part in English);
  • K without a vowel in front or at the end of words becomes "kah". In other situations, it sounds like the "c" in "cat", "cot"...;
  • H tends to extend the previous vowel as if breathy (or, if there is no vowel, the general consonant rule below applies). Starting with H, it tends to sound like a soft R sound, like in "ruRal";
  • W tends to sound like an "oo" (almost muted), not a strong "oo", reminiscent of the "wh" in the English word WHat;
  • G always has a soft "g" sound (like "gê" / soft "j"; e.g., "ga" sounds like "zha" / Portuguese "já");
  • J always has a hard "g" sound ("ji" sounds like "gee" in "geese" or "gui" in Portuguese);
  • X always sounds like "ks", similar to the "x" in NetfliX. With vowels, it sounds like "kss".
  • In general, consonants without vowels tend to carry a short neutral vowel sound attached (close to an "eh" sound).

For constructing words and verbs, we follow this format:

  1. GENDER-WORD-MAGNITUDE
    1. Plural (optional): WORD + WORD (repeats the word).
    2. GENDERS (optional):
      1. NI: feminine;
      2. NA: masculine.
    3. MAGNITUDE (optional, sometimes repeatable for emphasis or multiplier):
      1. XI: diminutive;
      2. XA: augmentative.
  2. PRONOUN - VERB - (TENSE or MODIFIER) - MAGNITUDE
    1. TENSES (optional for present tense):
      1. PA: past;
      2. FA: future;
      3. GA: gerund/continuous;
      4. LA: participle.
    2. MODIFIER (optional):
      1. KU: nominalizer (convert to noun).
    3. MAGNITUDE (optional, sometimes repeatable for emphasis or multiplier):
      1. XI: diminutive;
      2. XA: augmentative.

Gender in a word is optional, especially since not all words require it. You can make gender clear through sentence context. In older texts, you can still see the "meaning direction" suffix at the end (two letters), but it has currently fallen into disuse (AW for opposite meaning, OP for double opposite meaning, and AP for triple opposite meaning).

The pronoun attached to the verb is basically the subject, and tense is the verb tense. Both are optional and function similarly to Portuguese grammar.

All words can be "compacted" if the boundary letters are the same, such as "def'ury" which is "def fury". The pronunciation remains the same as when uncompacted.

Verbs can be directly nominalized without issues. A standalone verb that doesn't make sense in a sentence like "Ai tue ena" means "This is applicable" instead of "to apply" (which wouldn't make sense). However, to make it clearer, it is recommended to use the modifier "ku", resulting in "Ai tue enaku". Regarding augmentatives and diminutives, you can append "xi" or "xa" to the end of a word or verb, for example "uyokuxa" (big love / deep love).


Pronouns

Personal pronouns are very easy to use. It boils down to standard personal pronouns with one additional concept:

  • Ue: I / me;
  • Ae: you (singular, person spoken to, close by / next to me);
  • Pe: he/she/you (external, or addressing someone distant / far away);
  • Ua: we / us (all inclusive);
  • Sa: we / us (exclusive: speaker and their group addressing another group not included);
  • Va: you (plural, close by within a small radius, inclusive);
  • Ka: they / them (external or distant);

The cool part comes now: possessive pronouns (and others) are basically the personal pronouns, but reversed! So it's easy and intuitive.

  • Eu: my, mine, me;
  • Ea: your, yours (singular, here, nearby);
  • Ep: your, his, her, its (singular, distant, belonging to that far away person);
  • Au: our, ours (inclusive of everyone);
  • As: our, ours (exclusive of the listener's group);
  • Av: your, yours (plural, here, close circle);
  • Ak: their, theirs / your, yours (plural, distant, that group over there);

For demonstrative pronouns, we have something interesting:

  • Ai: this, that;
  • Wa: that over there;

It might seem quite confusing to think about the various "he/you" and "they/you" forms, but in reality it's extremely practical when you want to be specific. Have you ever said in a group "we're going to the movies" and left people confused about whether you meant just you and a friend or the whole group? That's where these variations help.

Ae and Ua are the most generic. When in doubt, use these. They can be ambiguous in some situations, but they work. Pe and Ka are for distant selection, for a person or group out of reach, such as when talking about someone or a group far away who likely won't hear you. Sa can be seen when, for example, you as a soccer team representative speak to another team: "We are going to win", you say. Who were you referring to? Your team, obviously—and using Sa makes that explicit. Va is even cooler because you are explicitly referring to everyone within your reach (whether by voice, proximity, or presence), including yourself. "We will survive" means you believe everyone who can see or hear you will survive, including yourself.

The words "this" and "that" (proximate) share the same word in this language, and for good reason. In most cases, people mix them up anyway and it makes no difference. With that in mind, both were mapped to the same word.

"PLOR" and "WALU", which meant "this/that" and "that over there", fell into disuse long ago. You might still find these words in older texts, but they are considered extremely obsolete.
